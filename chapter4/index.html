2.1 · Overview of using data

Variables, observations and variation

Data: facts and figures collected, analysed and summarised.
Variable: a characteristic that can take different values (a column).
Observation: the set of values for one entity (a row).
Variation: how a variable differs across observations. Descriptive analytics is about understanding it.
Random variable: a quantity whose value is not known with certainty, such as next month's sales.

2.2 · Types & sources of data

Population vs sample: draw your own sample

Sample size
Population: 700 ages at death. Population mean years. Draw samples and watch the sample means vary.
  • A population is all the elements of interest. A sample is a subset of it.
  • Random sampling gives a representative sample.
  • Bigger samples give sample means that sit closer to the population mean.

Where data come from

Experimental study: control or change a variable to see its effect (e.g., test two prices in two stores).

Observational study: no control, just observe. A survey is the most common type.

2.4 · Shape of a distribution

Skewness: which way does the tail point?

Skewleftright
MeanMedian
  • Skewed left: long tail on the left. Mean < median (e.g., exam scores when most do well).
  • Symmetric: mean ≈ median (e.g., heights).
  • Skewed right: long tail on the right. Mean > median (e.g., house prices, incomes).

2.6 · Measures of variability

Same average, very different risk

Annual payouts over 20 years, both averaging about $1,100
Which fund would you pick? Both pay about $1,100 on average. Fund A pays exactly that every year; Fund B swings from $0 to $2,500. Variability is risk.
2.6 · Variance, standard deviation, CV

Variance and standard deviation, step by step

s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)
s = √s²
CV = (s / x̄) × 100%

Sample data use n − 1 (Excel VAR.S, STDEV.S). For a whole population use VAR.P, STDEV.P.

2.7 · Analysing distributions

Percentiles and quartiles

p
  • The p-th percentile: about p% of values are below it.
  • Position L = (p/100)(n + 1), then interpolate between neighbours.
  • Quartiles: Q1 = 25th, Q2 = median = 50th, Q3 = 75th. IQR = Q3 − Q1.
=PERCENTILE.EXC(B2:B13,0.85)
=QUARTILE.EXC(B2:B13,1)

You do not need to sort the data before using the Excel functions.

2.7 · z-scores & the empirical rule

z-scores and the empirical rule

Within
Height
z = (x − x̄) / s
=STANDARDIZE(x,mean,sd)

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean.

For bell-shaped data: about 68% lie within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD, and almost all within ±3 SD.

Example: US adult male heights, mean 69.5 in, SD 3 in.

2.7 · Outliers & boxplots

Boxplots and outliers

  • Box = Q1 to Q3, with a line at the median.
  • Fences = Q1 − 1.5·IQR and Q3 + 1.5·IQR. Points beyond them are outliers.
  • Whiskers reach the most extreme values inside the fences.
  • For bell-shaped data, |z| > 3 also flags a possible outlier.
An outlier may be a typo, a value that doesn't belong, or a real extreme. Check it before deleting it!

Excel: select B1:B13 ▸ Insert ▸ Statistic Chart ▸ Box and Whisker.

2.8 · Correlation

Correlation: strength and direction, from −1 to +1

rxy = sxy / (sx sy)
=CORREL(x,y)
  • r near +1: strong positive linear relationship
  • r near −1: strong negative
  • r near 0: no linear relationship
  • Unlike covariance, r has no units, so it is comparable across data sets.
